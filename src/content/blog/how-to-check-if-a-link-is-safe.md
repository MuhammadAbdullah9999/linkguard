Clicking is the risky part. You can learn a lot about a link without ever opening it.

## 1. Read the domain, not the start of the URL

The part that matters is the domain just before the first single slash. In `https://paypal.com.account-verify.top/login`, the real domain is `account-verify.top`, not PayPal. Scammers put a trusted name at the front to fool a quick glance.

## 2. Watch for look-alikes

Swapped letters (`arnazon`), extra words (`amazon-support-help`), and odd endings such as `.top`, `.xyz` or `.click` are common in scam links. These endings are not always bad, but they deserve a second look.

## 3. Be careful with shortened links

Short links hide the destination. Before opening one, expand it or run it through a scanner that follows redirects.

## 4. Ask whether you expected it

Unexpected messages that create urgency, such as a locked account, an unpaid toll or a failed delivery, are the classic pattern. Go to the real site yourself instead.

## 5. Use a scanner

A link scanner follows the redirects and checks the final destination against known threat lists. LinkGuard does this on your phone and returns a clear verdict with the reasons.

## What a scanner cannot do

Brand-new scam pages may not be on any list yet. Treat a "safe" result as good news, not a guarantee. Never enter passwords or card details on a page you reached from an unexpected message.
